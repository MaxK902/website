Reymore and Hansen’s Study on Instrument-Specific Absolute Pitch Some musicians have an ability which allows them to hear any frequency and identify the musical pitch it most closely matches. This ability is called absolute pitch. It’s almost a superpower—those that have it can hear an airhorn, a cat’s purr, or the squeak of their shoe on the floor and instantly tell you which note it is. There’s a short window to learn this though—to develop true absolute pitch you have to be intensively taught and also be a young child, generally between three to five years old. Because of this limited window, absolute pitch is rare, with most estimates placing its frequency at less than 1% of the population. But there is a sort of second rate, diet version of absolute pitch that can be developed after early childhood called instrument-specific absolute pitch. Instrument-specific absolute pitch is the ability to determine any note played on a certain instrument just by hearing it. This is usually because of a musician’s intimate familiarity with an instrument. For example, an extremely experienced piano player may be able to simply hear a note played on a piano and identify its letter name (e.g. A, B flat, C sharp). The key is that the pianist can only do this on the piano, not any other instrument, which is different from true absolute pitch. Scientists don't know how and why this instrument-specific absolute pitch develops though. There are many theories, and in 2020, Lindsey Reymore of the University of Arizona and Niels Chr. Hansen of Arhus University set out to test four of them. For the study, Reymore and Hansen had two subjects who were both primarily oboe players. They chose oboe players specifically because oboes and other reeded instruments (clarinets, bassoons) have strong timbral differences between individual notes. Due to differences in the character of their overtones, an A and A sharp on an oboe sound quite different to the trained ear. This means it’s easier for oboists to develop instrument-specific absolute pitch—at least in theory. The two oboe players in the study participated in two separate experiments, with the first to determine whether the oboists actually had instrument-specific absolute pitch and the second to determine how instrument-specific absolute pitch works. The first experiment essentially had two parts. The oboe players were first played notes on the piano and asked to identify the notes’ letter name. A prerecorded piano note would play, the subjects would have a few seconds to think, and then they would put forth their guess as to what the pitch of the note was (e.g. A, B flat, C sharp). Then, the same process would occur except with prerecorded oboe notes being played instead of piano notes. If the subjects were more accurate with the oboe notes than with the piano notes, that would mean they have instrument-specific absolute pitch. If they were essentially equal in identifying piano notes versus oboe notes, that would mean they didn’t have instrument-specific absolute pitch. The results showed that oboist 1 was more than 40% more accurate in identifying oboe pitches as opposed to piano pitches, while oboist 2 was roughly the same in accuracy. This meant that for the second experiment, only oboist 1 would be used to determine the cause of instrument-specific absolute pitch, because oboist 2 didn’t actually have it. The second experiment was set up to test four theories of how instrument-specific absolute pitch works. The first and generally accepted theory among musicians is that musicians develop instrument-specific absolute pitch due to the unique timbres of their instrument, which they become increasingly familiar with over time. To test this, the experimenters played some recordings of regular oboe notes being played, and some recordings of oboe notes that had been shifted in pitch. The recordings were digitally shifted in such a way that the general timbre of the note would stay the same, while the actual frequency would be altered to match a different pitch. This is akin to the pitch of a speaking voice being raised when a video is sped up—the voice is still recognizable, but the frequency has been raised to create a slight chipmunk effect. This meant that any association the oboists had of notes and timbre would be ineffective, because the timbre they were used to associating with an A, for example, would end up being associated with a different note. If the oboists were more accurate in the regular notes than in the pitch-shifted ones, the theory would have credence. As for results, oboist 1 was more than 13% more accurate in identifying pitch in regular notes, meaning that this theory is probably correct. Another theory Reymore and Hansen tested was that musicians are able to develop instrument-specific absolute pitch due to motor associations in the brain. The idea is that when an experienced musician hears a note from their own instrument, the parts of the brain used in playing the instrument are activated and then through the motor processes the musician can visualize how the note is played and thus identify the note. So, if an oboist hears a note on an oboe, without thinking they put their fingers and mouth in the correct formation to play the note they hear, allowing them to perceive which note is being played. To test whether this was true, the experimenters developed an “interference” task—when some recordings of oboe notes were played, the subjects would chew gum and hold their arms in their hands so that they could not use motor imagery to determine the note, as their hands and mouth would be preoccupied. If the subjects were more accurate in identifying notes while not engaging in this interference task, that would mean this theory is correct. The results showed for oboist 1 that when there was no motor interference, she was over 12% more accurate in identifying pitch, meaning that this theory is also probably correct. There were two other theories that were tested but were found to have no material effect on pitch identification. There was the theory that pitch could be more readily identified on a musician’s own instrument as opposed to someone else’s. To test this, prerecorded notes from the oboist’s own oboes were played, and then prerecorded notes from other oboes were played. No real difference was found in identification ability for this scenario. There was also the theory that the oboists would be able to identify pitches more readily when they were the ones who had played the note. To test this, the experimenters had the subjects prerecord themselves playing notes. Then, they played back oboe notes that the subjects had played themselves and notes that they had not played. There was no real difference in identification between the two. Overall, Reymore and Hansen’s study found that motor imagery and timbral differences are the largest contributors to the development of instrument-specific absolute pitch. The scope of their study was limited though. They only tested on one person with instrument-specific absolute pitch—this tiny sample size means that their findings can’t really be accepted as gospel. But what the study does is lay the groundwork for future research on the subject with much larger sample sizes. Reymore and Hansen discussed the difficulties future researchers may face with this. For one, conducting the study in an efficient way would be difficult—to conduct these experiments on just two subjects took a matter of weeks. Another difficulty is that, simply put, there aren’t that many people out there to test on. Instrument-specific absolute pitch is likely more common than absolute pitch, but it’s still relatively rare. Both of these factors would have to be addressed in order to complete a larger, more comprehensive study of the subject. References Reymore, L. Hansen, N.C. (2020).
